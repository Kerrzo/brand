<ArticleSection dark id="software-as-an-art-form">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">01 / The beginning</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">Software was my art form.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">Programming was never just a job for me. It was a creative outlet.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I sometimes think of it like painting. You spend years learning the techniques, understanding the tools, and developing a feel for what works. Eventually, you stop thinking about every individual brushstroke and start thinking about what you're trying to create.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">And for the better part of thirty years, I was fortunate enough to make a career out of it.</p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection id="building-became-harder-than-imagining">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">02 / The friction</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">Somewhere along the way, building became harder than imagining.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">As my career progressed, so did the complexity of the software I worked on.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Frameworks became more sophisticated. Applications became larger. Expectations increased. There were architectures to maintain, dependencies to manage, security considerations, testing, deployment and a seemingly endless stream of things that needed attention.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">None of that was necessarily bad. Much of it was important engineering work, and I enjoyed learning how to do it well.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But something gradually changed.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I still had ideas. Lots of them.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Little applications I wanted to create. Experiments I wanted to try. Things I thought would be useful, or interesting, or simply beautiful to build.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But I knew what was involved.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">An idea that might take an afternoon to imagine could take weeks of evenings to implement properly. Before I could explore the interesting part, I'd need to set up the project, learn another framework, solve the inevitable technical problems and get everything working.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I still loved creating software.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I just didn't always have the energy to build the things I imagined.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">And when you spend your working days building software, the thought of spending your evenings battling another framework becomes a little less appealing.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Gradually, I stopped starting so many personal projects.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Not because I'd run out of ideas.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>Because the effort of bringing them to life had begun to outweigh the excitement of creating them.</strong></p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection dark id="ai-changed-the-equation">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">03 / The turning point</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">Then AI changed the equation.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">Like many developers, I initially approached AI-assisted programming with a mixture of curiosity and scepticism.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">The early tools were interesting, occasionally impressive, and frequently frustrating.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I experimented with them, but they didn't fundamentally change how I worked.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">That changed as the models became more capable.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">One of the turning points was a project involving Strategy Connect, a platform we'd developed and maintained for years.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">We'd wanted to rebuild parts of it for a long time. We knew the product, understood its problems and had plenty of ideas about what we'd do differently.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But the economics of rebuilding a working platform had never quite made sense.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">With AI-assisted development, we decided to experiment.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">What surprised me wasn't simply how quickly we could write code.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">It was how quickly we could turn an idea into something we could actually use.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I could describe an approach, have an AI agent implement it, run the software, see what worked and change direction.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">An idea no longer needed to survive days of implementation before I could find out whether it was any good.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>The feedback loop had changed.</strong></p>
    <p class="body-copy">And I began to realise what that meant for me personally.</p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection id="the-part-i-loved">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">04 / The work</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">I could get back to the part I loved.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">I've spent decades learning how to build software.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I understand architecture, engineering trade-offs, performance, testing and the countless details that separate software that works from software that works well.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I haven't stopped using those skills.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">If anything, I find myself drawing on them constantly.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But I'm applying them differently.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Instead of personally translating every decision into code, I spend more of my time deciding what the software should do, how it should behave and whether the result is good enough.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I work with AI coding agents. I give them direction, review their work, test what they've built and refine it.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Sometimes they make mistakes. Sometimes an implementation needs to be thrown away. Sometimes we go backwards before moving forwards.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I'm still responsible for the decisions.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But I'm no longer spending most of my creative energy on the mechanics of implementing them.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">And something unexpected happened.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>I started wanting to build things in my spare time again.</strong></p>
    <p class="body-copy">Not because I needed another business.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Not because someone was paying me.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Simply because I could imagine something interesting and experience it without spending weeks getting there.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">That feeling I'd had when I first discovered programming started coming back.</p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection dark id="the-craft-moved">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">05 / The craft</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">The craft hasn't disappeared. It's moved.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">Recently, I've been experimenting with a little iPhone video editor.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I wanted something that would let me shoot a video, make a few simple edits and share it without transferring everything to my computer and opening professional editing software.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">As I started using an early version, I found myself thinking about the interactions.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Why should changing a clip's speed require a complicated series of steps?</p>
    <p class="body-copy">What if you could simply stretch the clip to slow it down, or squeeze it to speed it up?</p>
    <p class="body-copy">What if adjusting the beginning of a clip automatically played a second of the previous clip and then continued across the cut, so you could immediately hear whether the edit felt natural?</p>
    <p class="body-copy">These are little things.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But they're the things I love thinking about.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I can explore an idea, have an agent implement it, try it on my phone and discover whether it actually feels right.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Then I can change it.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Perhaps the animation is too slow. Perhaps a control should disappear when it isn't needed. Perhaps an interaction that sounded clever turns out to be confusing.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">That's where I want to spend my time.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Not adding features for the sake of adding features, but refining an experience until it feels natural.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I've always admired applications where you can sense the care that went into them. Beautiful, restrained software that doesn't demand your attention. It simply lets you do what you came to do.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">That's the kind of software I want to create.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>And AI has given me a new way to pursue that craft.</strong></p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection id="where-this-leads">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">06 / Looking forward</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">I don't know where all this leads.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">There's plenty of speculation about what AI will mean for software development.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Will everyone build their own applications? Will traditional programming disappear? Will software become so easy to create that individual products lose their value?</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I don't know.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">And increasingly, I'm comfortable not knowing.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">What I do know is that I still love building things.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I want to keep developing serious products and solving difficult problems. But I also want to spend time creating small, thoughtful applications simply because I believe they should exist.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Things that make someone's day a little easier.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Things that are beautiful to use.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Things that people might recommend to a friend because they genuinely enjoy them.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I don't need every one of those ideas to become a business.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">If I spend the next five years creating a handful of applications that I'm proud of, and other people find them useful, I'll consider that time well spent.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Of course, getting people to discover and use software is its own challenge. That's something I'm looking forward to learning more about.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>But the motivation to create is there again.</strong></p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>

<ArticleSection dark id="still-a-software-craftsman">
  <ArticleTwoCol>
    <SectionLabel slot="label">07 / The outcome</SectionLabel>
    <h2 class="h-2">I'm still a software craftsman.</h2>
    <p class="body-copy">For a long time, writing code was how I expressed that craft.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Now, increasingly, it's through the things I imagine, the decisions I make, and the care I put into the finished experience.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">AI agents can write enormous amounts of code. They can solve difficult technical problems and implement ideas at a speed I couldn't have imagined a few years ago.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">But I still have to decide what is worth building.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">I still have to use the software, question it, refine it and decide when it feels right.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">And I still get that wonderful feeling when something that existed only in my imagination becomes real.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Perhaps that's what I've rediscovered.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>I never stopped loving software development. I just got tired of how much work it took to reach the part I loved.</strong></p>
    <p class="body-copy">AI has brought that part closer again.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">And after nearly thirty years of building software, I find myself excited about what I might create next.</p>
    <p class="body-copy">Not the income.</p>
    <p class="body-copy"><strong>The outcome.</strong></p>
  </ArticleTwoCol>
</ArticleSection>
